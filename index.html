Clock times below are starting points, not requirements. Every "deep-focus" block is sized at 30–45 minutes — enough for one lesson plus a few minutes of vocab reinforcement, since most lessons in your catalogs run 5–20 minutes anyway — and it can land wherever actually works that day: early morning before the job starts, the back half of your lunch on a day you get the full 2 hours, or evening. Pick whichever slot is realistic for that day rather than defaulting to "after work" every time.

Why it's built this way

01

One language per session, always. Spanish and Italian share enough vocabulary and grammar that studying them back to back makes your brain compete between them for the same word. Neither block below ever mixes the two, and their deep-focus days are never adjacent.

02

No language goes silent more than a day or two. A full week of "one language per day" leaves the other two untouched too long — that's where forgetting compounds. Every day now carries a 5-minute keep-warm touch (dashed border below) for a language that isn't today's main focus.

03

New vocab gets reviewed within about a day, not a week. Retention is best when the first review lands roughly 24 hours after first exposure, then a few days later, then weeks later. Lunch reviews lean on Quizlet's Learn / Spaced Practice mode rather than flipping cards front to back, so that timing happens automatically.

04

New material stays blocked; mixed review comes later. A brand-new verb tense or sound is still learned best in a focused block, not jumbled with other topics. Once a few sheets in a language are solid, an occasional mixed-review quiz across them (like GoSpeakSerbian's Review games) is where long-term retention actually gets tested.

05

Sessions sized to what you'll actually do. A schedule that assumes a spare hour every evening gets skipped the first busy week. 30–45 minutes, placeable in whichever window is open that day, is a block you can actually keep.

06

Spread out beats two days in a row, for a goal this long. A 2024 memory-and-cognition study found that for short-term recall, clustering reviews close together wins — but for retention intervals of weeks to months, evenly spaced or widening gaps beat clustered/back-to-back sessions by a wide margin. Since "fluent by 2028" is a multi-year goal, each language stays spread across the week rather than stacked on consecutive days.
